✉️ Senders (Gmail)

Who each email is from, at a glance, in your inbox and in opened emails, and automatic help with known phishing senders. How sender checks work

Show sender icons and domain labels
Each sender gets its site's icon and a label with its domain, colored only when it matters: ⛔ red when flagged, ⚠ amber for caution, ✓ green for senders you trust. Every other label is plain: no check can prove an email safe, and most mail comes from providers like Gmail that can't be judged by their domain. Point at a label for the details. Icons are fetched once from Google's icon service (only the site name is sent) and remembered for 30 days; a site without an icon gets a blank space, and in Offline only nothing is requested.
Stand-in icon for sites without their own
When a sender's site has no icon, its parent domain's is used (for alerts.brand.com, brand.com's), else one from DuckDuckGo's icon service (only the site name is sent, and only for such sites). They're checked again every week and replaced by the site's own icon as soon as it has one. Off: a blank space instead. Logging → Senders shows where each icon came from.
Sender photos and company logos
The picture Gmail itself shows beside a sender when you open an email, remembered and shown in your inbox list as a circle over the email's checkbox (point at the row, or tick it, and the checkbox is back). Your own organization: each colleague's own photo (never your company's logo). Another company: one logo for the whole company, shown for everyone there, learned from one email that passed Gmail's sender verification (DMARC) and came from one of its shared mailboxes (contact@, info@, support@, news@…) or carried a verified BIMI logo. A person's own photo at another company is never used; their company's logo is shown instead. Personal email services (Gmail, Outlook.com…) keep the site icon. A changed photo or logo replaces the old one; pictures are also checked again weekly.
Company logos from BIMI
The logo a company publishes for its email (BIMI), shown over the checkbox of every email from it in your inbox list, without opening any. Only for companies whose domain stops others sending in its name (DMARC quarantine or reject), and looked up for the company's main domain only. The logo is loaded from the company's own logo server, which sees your computer's address (as when a web page shows a picture); checked again weekly.
Size of sender pictures
Automatic follows Gmail's display density (Settings → Density): Default gets 36-pixel circles over each email's checkbox, Comfortable 32-pixel ones, and Compact a small icon beside the country flag instead. A sender with no photo, logo or site icon gets a colored circle with their initial (not in Compact).
Copy button for the sender's address
A small button after the sender's address in an opened email that copies the address, for example to report it or add it to a list. Works whatever the sender checks are set to.
Catch look-alike senders
In your inbox list, without opening anything: a sender whose domain imitates a popular site, a well-known brand, your own organization or a company your mail has verified (look-alike letters; a well-known brand plus a word like "billing"; on a brand-new domain, one letter off or a popular site's name plus such a word), and a brand's or a colleague's name on an address that isn't theirs. Clear imitations are flagged red and moved to Spam; weaker ones get amber. Before a brand's name on an address not known as the brand's counts against it, the domain is checked once more (online; at Offline only, a check made earlier still counts): public records that only the brand could have made (its DMARC reports, its name servers, a corporate registrar and a registrant naming it) show whether the domain is really the brand's. One that is isn't flagged; one that looks like it gets amber instead of red; otherwise it's red as before. Uses two public lists of popular sites (Chrome UX Report and Tranco, updated monthly) and, for near matches and that check, the domain's public registry record.
Catch senders using your own name
In your inbox list, without opening anything: an email whose sender name is your own email address, or your mailbox name (the part before the @: written exactly as in your address, or in any case if your address is at Gmail, Outlook.com or another big provider, where it's nobody's real name), but which comes from someone else's address. Scams do this so the email looks like a notice about your own account. Flagged red and moved to Spam. Shared mailbox names (info, office, support…), your own organization's senders, and an address that contains your mailbox name (likely your other account) are left alone.
Weigh new and outside senders (experimental)
Two small weights that count only together with other warning signs: an email from outside your organization (when your account has its own domain, such as a school's), and the first email from an address. Never amber on their own. Addresses are remembered on this computer only as scrambled codes (hashes), and only after 14 days of learning does a sender count as new.
Catch “call this number” scams
A subject with a phone number to call about a purchase or charge you didn't make (“If this purchase was not made by you, contact support at…”) is flagged red from the inbox list and moved to Spam, whoever sent it: attackers type such text into names that companies' own systems mail out, so it can arrive from Microsoft, PayPal or Apple's real addresses. A number with only “call” or “support” is a caution. In an opened email, “call this number if you didn't make this purchase” is a caution too, as are look-alike “mathematical” letters (𝐔𝐒𝐃). Works in Outlook too, in opened emails.
Show the sender's country
A flag for the country of the server that sent the email, from Gmail's own delivery record (read emails only, and unread ones in the Spam folder; inbox emails are never opened to look; at most two at a time). Otherwise the country the sender's domain ending names (such as .de), or where its website is hosted. Only the server's address is looked up, at country.is, once a month at most; in Offline only only the domain ending is used.
Check sender domains for threats
The sender's domain is checked with the same security services as links (only its name, for example example.com) and against the phishing lists. Gmail, Outlook.com and other big providers' domains, and your own organization's, are never judged as a whole.
Check sender addresses against phishing lists
The address is compared on this device with a community list of scam and phishing senders (sefinek/Blacklisted-Emails, downloaded with the phishing lists, at most once a day). A malformed address (a disguised encoded part, more than 64 characters before the @, two @) is flagged too.
Move emails from flagged senders to Spam
Only flagged emails (sender's exact address on the community scam-sender list; domain blocked by a security service or nonexistent; malformed address; or enough signs of forgery in the email itself), never ones that are unverified, caution, trusted or clean. Gmail's own Report spam is used, only for emails in your inbox (and search results, below), and never while you're typing or have emails selected. While an email waits its turn, its row says Moving to Spam… and can't be clicked by accident; its Move now moves it immediately, its Keep it leaves it in the inbox for good. If you take an email back out of Spam, it stays where you put it. Your organization's phishing-training emails, trusted senders and mail from your own organization's domain are never moved.
Also move flagged emails found by a search
Flagged emails in search results are moved to Spam too, as in the inbox: a search shows your mail wherever it's filed (archived or under a label), not only the inbox. Never a search in Spam, Trash, Sent, Drafts, chats or everywhere (in:spam, in:trash, in:sent, in:anywhere…), and never conversations you wrote in. Needs Move emails from flagged senders to Spam.
Tell me when emails are moved
A short notice at the top of Gmail says how many emails were moved, with a Review link to your Spam folder. Moves are also listed in the toolbar popup's recent activity and in Logging.
Flag dangerous emails in the Spam folder
In Spam, emails the same checks would move there are marked red, whether this extension moved them or not, with the reason; unread ones are checked too (their headers are read). Anything not red there was put in Spam by Gmail or by you. If reading an unread email's headers ever marks it read, the extension stops doing it and says so in Logging.

Emails from these addresses (name@example.com) or whole domains (example.com, which includes its subdomains) are never flagged or moved to Spam, and their label shows ✓ Trusted. Links in them are still checked. Entries from your organization are marked and can't be removed here; your organization's own list of phishing senders still wins.

How it works

When you open an email in Gmail (or another supported webmail: Outlook, Proton, Yahoo, AOL, Zoho, iCloud, Fastmail or GMX), the extension looks at every link and works out where it really goes, warns you about dangerous or misleading ones, and quietly removes invisible tracking images. It needs no setup.

A helper, not a guarantee. S.P.R.U.C.E+ makes a best effort to point out warning signs, but it can miss dangerous emails and links, and it can flag genuine ones. It is one small layer of protection: keep your webmail's own spam filtering, your organization's email security, your browser's protections and your own judgment. When in doubt, don't click: contact the sender another way.
1. Email openedLinks and images in the message are read in your browser.
2. Decoded on your deviceSecurity and tracking wrappers (Proofpoint, Microsoft SafeLinks, Barracuda, Google, Facebook, ad and affiliate trackers and 50+ more) are unpacked locally.
3. Short links looked upIf the destination is still hidden behind a short link (bit.ly…), it is looked up online. Never for one-time or email-provider links.
4. CheckedThe destination's site name is checked against security services, and the link is checked for tricks such as text that shows a different site.
5. ShownLinks that pass the checks point straight to their destination. Ones flagged as dangerous are marked in red and ask before opening.

When you send an email (Gmail), the links you wrote are checked before it goes: typos such as “.con” or “http//”, whether each website exists and works, and whether it's a look-alike, phishing or malware site. If something's wrong, you see which links and why, and choose Go back and fix or Send anyway.

In your Gmail inbox, every sender gets its site's icon, a country flag and a label with its domain, colored by a check of the sender: its address against phishing-sender lists and its domain against the same security services as links. Emails from a flagged sender are moved to Spam for you, once, with a short notice to review them. See Sender icons & Spam.

The row highlighter marks the email under your mouse with a soft color and an outline, so a click opens the email you meant, and the emails you've ticked in a second color (blue by default), so the two are never confused. Both colors can be changed in Settings → Row highlighter. If another extension does the same job (for example another sender-icon or row-highlighter extension), you're warned and can turn one of them off with one click: see Settings → Other extensions.

Which of these work on which webmail is shown in Which features work where. Link, QR-code and tracking-pixel checks work on every supported webmail, and so do sender labels and checks (basic on sites without an entry of their own). Moving to Spam and the Send check run on Gmail today and reach other sites as their entries are filled in and tested.

Everything you see is also available in this window: the Link Inspector shows each step for any link you paste, and Logging holds the application log (every problem and event), the history of checked links and the URL cache. Technical readers will find every detail in the architecture deep dive.

Signs of a forged email

Besides the sender's lists and domain, each email is looked at for signs that genuine mail systems never produce. Each sign has a weight; strong signs flag an email on their own, weaker ones add up (enough of them flag it, a few make it Caution). The label and banner list every sign found.

  • A malformed sender address (strong): a disguised encoded part such as =?UTF-8?B?…?=, more than 64 characters before the @, two @, or an invalid domain.
  • A sender domain that doesn't exist (strong): genuine mail can't come from it, and replies can't reach it. A company subdomain with no DNS records of its own (some companies send from one, such as hello.blink.com) counts only when the company's own domain doesn't exist either; otherwise the company's domain is checked instead.
  • Its links (opened emails): when all its links, or two or more, are flagged as dangerous (for example phishing pages in cloud storage), the email is flagged; one dangerous link among others makes it Caution. So a red-linked email no longer shows a green ✓ sender.
  • Its attachments (opened emails): an attachment flagged as dangerous (strong); an attachment without a name (“noname”).
  • Its headers (opened emails, and read emails in the list): a failed sender verification (DMARC) when the sender's domain asks for such mail to be treated as spam or rejected, fake delivery records copied from older emails (dated more than a week before it arrived), a MIME version other than 1.0, an encoding that doesn't exist, a made-up mailing-list ID, a misspelled “Recived” line, web-page code in the address lines, an “In-Reply-To” line holding an address, a fake Gmail (or other big provider's) signature, a mark from another mail system that already found it was spam, an unsubscribe link or tracking image on the sender's own computer (127.0.0.1), headers claiming several unrelated sending services at once, unfilled bulk-mail placeholders, repeated From/Subject/Date lines, two different Content-Length sizes, a “To” line that shows one address but is addressed to another, a subject padded with blank space or random capitals, or ending in filler and random letters (“_______kvksrphdly”).
  • “Call this number” text: a phone number to call about a purchase you didn't make, in the subject (strong) or in an opened email (caution), even in an email genuinely sent by a company; look-alike “mathematical” letters (𝐔𝐒𝐃). Setting Catch “call this number” scams.
  • Its sender name: padded with symbols, rows of quotes, blank space or invisible characters; or your own email address or mailbox name on someone else's address (strong, seen in the inbox list without opening; setting Catch senders using your own name).

Look-alike senders and brand names

Setting Catch look-alike senders. Seen in the inbox list, without opening anything:

  • A domain that imitates another: look-alike letters (pаypal with a Cyrillic “а”), a brand domain worn at the start (paypal.com.verify-acct.net), a well-known brand plus a word like “billing” (paypal-billing.com), or an imitation of your own organization's address (red, moved to Spam). One letter off a popular site, or a popular name plus such a word: red only when the domain was registered in the last 90 days; amber for the built-in brands otherwise.
  • A brand's name on an address that isn't the brand's (“PayPal Service” <help@random-shop.com>): red, moved to Spam.
  • A legal third party writing about a brand (“Amazon Return Policy Litigation Settlement Administrator” <donotreply@returnsettlement.com>): amber, never moved. Class-action settlement and court notices name the company they're about; only names with legal words (settlement, litigation, class action, lawsuit, “in re”, “v.”) count, so “Amazon Support” stays red. Fake settlement notices exist too: check the case on the court's or an official website before entering personal or payment details.
  • A colleague's name on an outside address: amber (colleagues sometimes write from a personal address).

Before a brand's name counts against an address, its domain is checked once more. Companies send from more domains than their main one (Amazon Music from amazonmusic.com, Facebook from facebookmail.com), so when the name claims a brand on a domain not known as the brand's, public records are read first (online; at Offline only, what was read earlier, within 30 days, still counts, and nothing new is asked):

When an email is moved to Spam

  • Only when it's flagged. Unverified, caution, trusted and clean ones are never moved. A conversation is moved only if every other sender in it is flagged, so one flagged reply never sends your colleagues' messages to Spam.
  • Only from your inbox (including its category tabs) and from search results, as a list or opened. Never from a search in Spam, Trash, Sent, Drafts, chats or everywhere, and never from Spam, Trash, Sent, labels or All mail. Moving from search results can be switched off: Settings → Senders → Also move flagged emails found by a search. With Multiple Inboxes or Priority Inbox sections, or the conversation open beside the list (split view), emails are moved only when you open them in the normal view.
  • Gmail's own Report spam is used, exactly as if you clicked it; if Gmail asks “Report spam and unsubscribe?”, only plain Report spam is chosen (unsubscribing would confirm your address to a phisher). Anything unexpected is cancelled.
  • Never while you're busy: not while you're typing, have a menu or dialog open, or have selected emails yourself (they would be reported too). After your last click or key it waits 10 seconds, so a move never replaces the Undo of something you just did; after the mouse last moved over the list, 2 seconds, so rows never shift under a pointer in motion. It waits and tries again the moment you pause.
  • While it waits, the row says so and is held still: its subject is struck through and a small banner shows Moving to Spam… and why it's waiting, when it is. Clicks on the row are ignored (the banner flashes instead), so the email can't be opened, and a row about to disappear can't be clicked by accident. The banner's Move now moves it immediately — that click is your own request, so nothing waits; Keep it leaves it in the inbox for good, remembered like an email you took out of Spam. Only clicks are guarded: Gmail's own keyboard shortcuts (j/k, then Enter or o) can still open the email — opened, it keeps its red warning banner, and it is still moved, from the opened conversation or once you're back in the list.
  • Once per conversation. A conversation is remembered just before it's moved. If you take an email out of Spam (Not spam or Move to Inbox, or Undo right away), whether the extension or Gmail put it there, it's never moved again (those you take out are kept in their own list, so later moves never push them out). To stop a sender being flagged, add it to Settings → Senders → Trusted senders. There is no list of senders to block: report anything missed with Gmail's own Report spam. Every move, failed move and rescue is listed in Logging → Moved to Spam, with the account it happened in; when you take a moved email back out of Spam, its own line is marked Taken out of Spam by you with the date and time, so each email keeps one line with its whole story.
  • Never your organization's phishing-training emails. With simulation link domains set, emails are moved only once opened (their links show whether they're training); with header rules, an unread email, whose headers aren't read, is left alone, and never emails from your own organization's domain, unless you or your organization listed that exact address (for example a hacked account).
  • Gmail, Outlook.com, Yahoo and other big providers' domains, and big companies' own domains, are never judged as a whole: there, only exact addresses on a list count.

Privacy

Nothing about your emails is sent anywhere. The checks send only: each sender's domain to the threat-check services (as for links), its site name to Google's icon service (and, for a site without an icon of its own, to DuckDuckGo's), and a sending server's IP address to country.is. Results are remembered (verdicts 6 hours, icons and countries 30 days), so the inbox shows them at once without new requests. Emails' headers are read from Gmail itself (your own session). Your privacy

The extension has no account, no analytics and no server of its own. Your email's text, subject, sender's address, recipients and attachments are never sent anywhere. (In Gmail, the sender's domain can be checked like a link's site name; see below.)

Never leaves your browser

  • Email content. Only link addresses, link text and image addresses are examined, locally.
  • One-time links (password reset, sign-in, verification, unsubscribe): never sent, cached or logged in full.
  • Email-provider tracking links (Mailchimp, SendGrid, Klaviyo…): they identify you personally, so they are never sent to anyone.
  • Full links to threat-check services: they receive only the site name.
  • Your own account address (from the webmail's page title) is written next to log entries so you can tell your accounts apart. It stays in this browser's storage with the logs, is deleted with them, and is never sent anywhere.

8. Tips

  • Lock only what matters. Settings you leave out of the policy keep their defaults and remain adjustable by users.
  • API keys for your own resolver or unshortener can be delivered by policy (managedDohHeaders, managedUnshortenerHeaders); users see them masked and they are never included in backups.
  • No third-party requests at all: set managedEnableThirdPartyResolution, managedEnableDnsThreatCheck, managedEnableRemoteListSync and managedEnablePhishingFeeds to false; nothing then leaves the browser.
  • Network filters: allow the services listed under every network request, or deliver your own resolver and unshortener by policy.
  • Troubleshooting: if a setting isn't applied, compare its name with the settings reference (names are case-sensitive) and look at chrome://policy / edge://policy, which marks invalid values.

  • managedPhishSimulationRules (Gmail) lists your phishing-simulation (security training) emails, for example from KnowBe4 or Wizer, by the header the vendor adds, a secret value in it, and/or the vendor's sending addresses. In Gmail, a matching email is left completely unchecked: no link is looked up (which the vendor could record as a click), no tracking pixel is blocked and no warning is shown. Use a secret value or the sending addresses: a public header name alone could be copied by real phishing.
"managedPhishSimulationDomains": ["ssuport.com", "the-verification.com"], "managedPhishSimulationRules": [ { "name": "KnowBe4", "header": "X-KB4TOKEN", "value": "YOUR-KSAT-TOKEN" }, { "name": "Wizer", "header": "X-TestPhish", "ips": ["<Wizer's sending addresses>"] } ]

Best practice: use both identifiers: the vendor's link domains (every webmail) and a header rule with a secret value (Gmail). List only domains the vendor owns, and keep the list current. ADMIN-GUIDE.md has a best-practice checklist and step-by-step setups for KnowBe4 and Wizer, with links to their documentation.

8. Tips

  • Lock only what matters. Settings you leave out of the policy keep their defaults and remain adjustable by users.
  • API keys for your own resolver or unshortener can be delivered by policy (managedDohHeaders, managedUnshortenerHeaders); users see them masked and they are never included in backups.
  • No third-party requests at all: set managedEnableThirdPartyResolution, managedEnableDnsThreatCheck, managedEnableRemoteListSync and managedEnablePhishingFeeds to false; nothing then leaves the browser.
  • Network filters: allow the services listed under every network request, or deliver your own resolver and unshortener by policy.
  • Troubleshooting: if a setting isn't applied, compare its name with the settings reference (names are case-sensitive) and look at about:policies, which marks errors in the file.

Deploying in organizations

S.P.R.U.C.E+ isn't in Apple's App Store yet (it is in the Chrome Web Store, Microsoft Edge Add-ons and Firefox Add-ons), so for Safari you build it yourself for now. In Safari an extension comes inside a small Mac (or iPhone/iPad) app. You build the app once from this extension, sign it, and install it on your devices; device management can then keep the extension turned on and allowed on your webmail. Safari has no extension policies, so settings can't be set or locked centrally (see What can be managed).

Before you start. You need a Mac with Xcode (free from the App Store) to build the app, and an Apple Developer account to sign it for other computers. Test with a small pilot group first.

1. Build the app

  1. On a Mac, run tools/build-safari.sh from this extension's folder. It uses Apple's converter (xcrun safari-web-extension-converter) to wrap the extension in an Xcode project and builds it. Or let GitHub build it: the Safari build workflow does the same on a GitHub Mac and offers the unsigned app for download.
  2. To try it on one Mac without signing: in Safari, Settings → Advanced → Show features for web developers, then Develop → Allow Unsigned Extensions (asked again each time Safari starts), open the app once, and turn the extension on in Safari → Settings → Extensions.

2. Sign and distribute it

  • Mac App Store or TestFlight: open the generated Xcode project, choose your team under Signing & Capabilities, and use Product → Archive to upload it. Apps from the store can be assigned to devices with Apple Business Manager or Apple School Manager.
  • Directly (Developer ID): archive the app, sign it with your Developer ID and have Apple notarize it, then deploy it with your device-management tool (Jamf, Intune, Mosyle…).

3. Turn it on everywhere

Apple's Safari extensions management declarative configuration (macOS 15, iOS 18 and iPadOS 18 or later; supervised devices) can set the extension to Always On, control Private Browsing, and allow it on your webmail sites (for example mail.google.com) so people aren't asked. The extension is identified as Identifier (TeamIdentifier); get it with codesign -dv on the extension inside the app's PlugIns folder. The app must be installed on the device.

4. Website access

Safari asks each person before an extension can read a website. Without the configuration above, people choose Always Allow on This Website for their webmail the first time. The extension also downloads its lists and runs its threat checks from its background page (the services listed under Permissions). If Safari asks about those sites, allow them; otherwise the lists and checks can't update, and Settings → Logging says so.

5. Threat checks with Google Web Risk

Google's Web Risk API checks full addresses against Google's lists of phishing and malware pages (the data behind Google Safe Browsing). It needs a Google Cloud project with billing enabled; check Google's current Web Risk pricing first. Note: unlike the built-in checks, which only send site names, Web Risk receives the full link (one-time links are never sent).

  1. In the Google Cloud console, sign in with your Google account and create or choose a project.
  2. APIs & Services → Library: find Web Risk API and click Enable.
  3. APIs & Services → Credentials → Create credentials → API key. Open the key, choose Restrict key, and under API restrictions allow only the Web Risk API.
  4. In this extension: Settings → Your own services → Custom threat resolver:
    Address: https://webrisk.googleapis.com/v1/uris:search?threatTypes=MALWARE&threatTypes=SOCIAL_ENGINEERING&threatTypes=UNWANTED_SOFTWARE&uri={url} Method: GET Request headers: X-Goog-Api-Key: YOUR_API_KEY Flag field: (leave empty: Google's replies are recognized automatically)
    Click Save service, then Test resolver. Matches show as, for example, "Phishing (Google Web Risk)".
  5. To give it to everyone, each person sets it up once (API keys are never included in backups, so they aren't shared through a backup file).

Google Safe Browsing (v4, threatMatches:find) is also understood, but Google limits it to non-commercial use; organizations should use Web Risk. Short links: Google has no API to expand them; use your own lookup service (Limitations).

6. What can be managed

In Safari, nothing inside the extension can be set or locked by an administrator: there is no extension policy (Chrome and Firefox have one, and their builds of this extension use it). What you can do:

  • Same settings for everyone: set the extension up once, use Back up, and have each person Restore the file. API keys are never included in backups.
  • Keep it on: the Safari extensions management configuration above (Always On, website access).
  • Not available in Safari: locked settings, the administrator-only options (hiding the popup's buttons, phishing-simulation rules for security-training emails) and the organization's own services delivered with their keys. Security-training emails from vendors such as KnowBe4 may therefore be flagged; add the vendor's sending domain to Trusted senders and its link domains to the Allowlist before making the backup you share.

7. Tips

  • No third-party requests at all: set the privacy level to Offline only before making the backup you share.
  • Network filters: allow the services listed under every network request.
  • Troubleshooting: in Safari, Develop → Web Extension Background Content opens the extension's background page with its console; Settings → Logging shows what the extension did.

Links

  • One-time link protection: password-reset, sign-in and unsubscribe links are never sent anywhere, cached or logged in full, so they can't be used up.
  • Email click trackers (SendGrid, Brevo, Klaviyo, Mailchimp, HubSpot…) recognized and never sent anywhere automatically; 🔍 reveals the destination on request.
  • Threat checks of every destination with free filtering resolvers: Quad9, Cloudflare Security and Family, Control D, optionally DNS4EU, with AdGuard as backup, or your own service.

  • Look-alike domains that imitate well-known brands: look-alike letters, one-letter typos, "brand.com.something.xyz" and brand names next to words like "login".

Companies' own domains count as one (Gmail and Google, Microsoft and aka.ms, Bank of America and bofa.com…), and so does an address under a company's own ending (go.amex for American Express, blog.google for Google), from ICANN's list of the endings only one company may use, downloaded monthly; a link to such an ending is never red, as no one else can have an address there. Text showing the site of the email's own sender, verified by Gmail (DMARC), is amber at most, wherever the link goes.   • Cloud-storage phishing pages (Google Cloud Storage, Firebase, S3, Azure, R2, IPFS) flagged, with hidden "#" data decoded; 🔍 reads the page's code (without running it) to find where it redirects.
  • Checking links before sending (Gmail): when you press Send (or Ctrl/⌘+Enter), the links you wrote are checked for typos in their ending or beginning, websites that don't exist or don't answer, text showing another site, and look-alike, phishing or malware sites. Problems are listed with Go back and fix or Send anyway; emails without links send at once.
  • Sender checks (Gmail): company-only links (c.gle…) whose destination is unknown are trusted only in email Gmail verified as sent by that company directly to you; sender names impersonating brands, brand domains failing verification, look-alike sender domains and scam text in genuine company notifications are flagged; a brand's name on one of its own other domains (shown by public records) isn't. Gmail's verdict is read from the message itself, only when needed.
  • Sign-in links that redirect elsewhere (real Microsoft or Google sign-in pages used as redirectors) are marked Caution.
  • Code links (javascript:, data:, vbscript:, blob:, file:) disabled.
  • Your own lists: sites you always trust (allowed) or always block, with the destination of blocked links hidden until you choose to reveal it.
  • Tracking parameters (utm_… and similar) removed from destinations.

Design principles

  • Local first. Everything that can be worked out on the device is: 50+ wrapper formats are decoded offline before any network request is considered.
  • The destination is never contacted. The extension never requests a link's own server. Short links are expanded by a lookup service; threat checks send only a hostname to DNS resolvers.
  • Fail safe, never fail "safe". Anything that can't be checked is "not verified", never "safe". Unreadable replies count as no answer. A decoder that doesn't recognize a format leaves the link unchanged.
  • One-time links are protected. One-time links are never sent anywhere, cached or logged in full, and only gateway decoders that return the complete original URL may touch them.
  • Untrusted input stays untrusted. Email content, community lists, backup files and custom-service replies are validated, size-limited and parsed defensively.
  • No third-party code. No libraries, no build step, no remote code, no analytics. Every line that runs is in the package.
  • Efficient by design. Nothing runs in the background on a timer except the daily list update and cleanup. Settings are read once and kept in memory until they change; each site is checked once even when many links point to it; log, history and cache writes for a whole email are combined into one storage write; the toolbar badge updates once per burst; and state for messages Gmail has closed is released.

The mail engine and webmail entries LIVE

The link pipeline, step by step

From the moment a message opens to the moment a link is marked. Function names refer to the source files.

  1. Detect content.js · MutationObserver A single observer on document.body watches for the webmail inserting a message body (its entry's body selector; Gmail's is .ii.gt) and, where the entry describes one, inbox rows. Bursts of changes are debounced (350 ms), then the scan runs once.
  2. Prepare unshortenLinks Settings are fetched from the service worker (getSettings). Hidden links are disabled, tracking pixels handled, then every <a> with an http(s) host other than mail.google.com is sent once per unique address (resolveUrl), together with its visible text, image alt text and aria-label (used for one-time detection).
  3. Prepare unshortenLinks Settings are fetched from the background page (getSettings). Hidden links are disabled, tracking pixels handled, then every <a> with an http(s) host other than mail.google.com is sent once per unique address (resolveUrl), together with its visible text, image alt text and aria-label (used for one-time detection).
  4. Blocklist resolveLink · blocklistHit If the link's own host is blocklisted, the answer is immediate: flagged, destination hidden, nothing decoded, checked or sent. A later reveal re-enters the pipeline with reveal: true (as does revealing an email tracking link).
  5. Allowlist The link is decoded offline; if both it and its destination are allowlisted, it is left untouched.
  6. Cache Ordinary links are answered from the local cache when possible; the blocklist is re-applied to cached destinations.
  7. Decode offline decoders.js · unwrapNestedUrl Up to six layers are peeled, trying gateway decoders first, then the redirect table, JSON payloads, base64, query parameters and path-embedded URLs. For one-time links only gateway decoders run.
  8. One-time check isSingleUseToken The outer link, the decoded link (twice URL-decoded) and the link text are matched against the patterns listed below. A one-time link skips straight to the threat check with its full address withheld from every service.
  9. Eligibility networkEligibility If the destination is still hidden, the rules below decide whether it may be looked up online.
  10. Look up lookupShortLink Custom unshortener first (if set), then unshorten.me (if allowed). The result is re-checked for one-time patterns.
  11. Clean cleanTrackingParams utm_*, fbclid, gclid, mc_cid, mc_eid and _bta_tid are removed, only when present (the URL is otherwise left byte-for-byte).
  12. Assess assessSafety · checkDomainSafety Blocklist first, then the destination and (if different) the link's own host are checked in parallel; the worse result wins, so the verdict is valid whichever address the click actually reaches.
  13. Record Conclusive results are cached; inconclusive, blocklisted and revealed ones never are. History and the activity feed are updated; problems go to the error log.
  14. Show content.js · detectLinkProblem · applyLinkStyles Misleading-link checks run against the visible text, then the link is rewritten or marked. The sender's original styling and markup are saved and restored exactly when highlights are switched off.

Offline decoders LIVE

Each decoder recognizes its format by the parsed hostname (exact host or real subdomain) plus the path, never by text elsewhere in the URL, so https://evil.example/?x=urldefense.com is not treated as Proofpoint. Decoded results must be valid http(s) URLs. Order matters: the first decoder that matches wins, then the loop repeats on the result.

One-time link detection LIVE

A link is treated as one-time if any of these regular expressions match. The patterns deliberately err on the side of caution: a false positive only means a link isn't looked up.

Short-link lookup rules

Evaluated in order by networkEligibility; the first rule that applies decides.

  1. "Look up short links online" off → never.
  2. Email-provider tracker → only when the user reveals it with its 🔍 (and "Offer to reveal email tracking links" is on). Never automatically: it is unique to the recipient, following it registers a click, and it may wrap an unsubscribe or sign-in link.
  3. Blocklisted host → only when the user reveals it.
  4. Built-in public shortener → yes.
  5. Domain only on a synced community list → only if the URL has a short-link shape (no query or fragment, one or two path segments of up to 32 characters).
  6. Unknown domain that looks like a short link (two-label domain with a two-letter country ending, one 4–12 character code with digits or mixed case) → yes.
  7. Anything else → no request.

The lookup chain is the custom unshortener (if configured), then unshorten.me unless its fallback is switched off. Custom replies are read from the configured field or common fields (resolved_url, long_url, final_url, destination…, one level deep) or a plain-text URL; a reply that merely echoes the request is ignored.

Threat-check engine LIVE

For each hostname, a non-filtering reference lookup and all enabled services run in parallel. Filtering resolvers signal a block by answering with a sinkhole address (0.0.0.0, 127.0.0.1); Quad9 answers NXDOMAIN, which is told apart from a genuinely missing domain by the reference lookup.

  • unsafe if any security or content service blocks it; unknown ("not verified") if no security service answered or the domain doesn't exist; otherwise safe.
  • The AdGuard backup is asked only when no primary security service answered; its blocks are ignored for known email-tracker and shortener hosts, which it blocks by design.
  • Conclusive verdicts (safe from the primary services, or unsafe) are kept in memory for (at most 1,000 sites, cleared whenever settings change); unknown is never kept. Links to the same site checked at the same moment share one round of requests. A custom resolver that receives full URLs is cached per URL rather than per host.
  • Custom-resolver replies are understood for DNS-over-HTTPS JSON, Safe Browsing (matches, {} = clean), URLhaus (query_status), simple malicious/blocked fields, or a configured flag field. Anything else (e.g. an invalid-key error) counts as no answer.
  • Timeouts: 6 s per DNS request, 10 s per link lookup, 15 s per list download. Requests are made without cookies.

Misleading-link and pixel heuristics

  • Misleading links (detectLinkProblem): user:password@ hosts; IPv4 or IPv6 literal hosts; any xn-- (punycode) label; and link text that names a domain whose registrable domain differs from the destination's. Registrable domains are approximated without the full public-suffix list (two-letter country endings with common second levels such as co.uk). Text that ends in a file extension (report.pdf), equals the link's own domain, or points through an unresolved built-in tracker is not compared. Two domains of the same company are consistent: one table of company domains serves the link and the sender checks, and so are an ending only one company can register (ICANN's "brand" endings, from ICANN's list of endings, downloaded monthly) and a domain the extension knows to be that company's whose name is one of the endings of the same registry (sameCompanyByEnding: americanexpress.com and go.amex, as .amex and .americanexpress have one registry). A link to such an ending that can't be tied to the text's company is amber at most: no one else can have an address there. A red finding about text showing a brand's site is looked at again when Gmail's DMARC result is read (verifiedSenderLinkProblem): if it passed for that same company and the link isn't in a quoted part, the link becomes amber. A domain that only carries the name of Google, Amazon or PayPal under another ending is amber until the background shows it is theirs (companyDomainEvidence: on both popular-site lists and enforcing DMARC, or proven by the brand-ownership check); then the link isn't marked. At Offline only, ownership answers and DMARC policies stored from earlier lookups still count; nothing new is asked. Each of these steps has its own switch (COMPANY_MATCHING in shared.js).
  • Hidden links: inline font-size that parses to 0, or opacity ≤ 0.01.
  • Tracking pixels: the original image URL is recovered from Gmail's proxy (after #). An image is a pixel if its host is a built-in tracker or blocklisted, its host or path follows tracker conventions, or its size marks it as a beacon: any dimension over 20 px is never a pixel; ≤ 3×8, area ≤ 24 px², a single dimension ≤ 3 px, inline-hidden or inside a collapsed zero-height box is. Images whose size is unknown are re-checked when they finish loading. Allowlisted hosts are skipped.

Built on

Third-party lists are downloaded at most once a day (the popular-site lists once a month) and used as published. Thank you to everyone who maintains them.

  • PeterDaveHello/url-shorteners and boutetnico/url-shorteners: community-maintained lists of URL shorteners (see each project for its license). The bundled starter list (shortener-snapshot.txt) is a copy of them.
  • OpenPhish community phishing feed, used under the OpenPhish Terms of Use.
  • Phishing Army blocklist (phishing.army project), licensed CC BY-NC 4.0; used unmodified apart from leaving out major sites.
  • Sefinek Blacklisted-Emails: community list of scam and phishing sender addresses (MIT), for the sender checks.
  • The Chrome UX Report top sites (data by Google, CC BY 4.0), from the zakird/crux-top-lists copy by Zakir Durumeric, and the Tranco research ranking (KU Leuven and others; Le Pochat et al., NDSS 2019): popular sites protected from look-alike senders.
  • ICANN's list of generic top-level domains: which internet endings only one company may use (its brand endings, such as .amex), for the link checks. The bundled starter copy (company-endings.json) is made from it by tools/refresh-snapshot.js.
  • unshorten.me: short-link expansion.
  • Sender details: site icons from Google's and DuckDuckGo's icon services, server countries from country.is, and domain registration dates through IANA's registry directory (RDAP).
  • Quad9, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 for Families, Control D free DNS, DNS4EU and AdGuard DNS: free public threat-filtering resolvers.
  • pdf.js by Mozilla (Apache-2.0), bundled for reading PDF attachments (license in lib/PDFJS-LICENSE).
  • Trocker (Apache-2.0): inspiration for tracking-pixel detection.
  • Tracking-pixel patterns informed by the Ugly Email and Leave Me Alone tracker lists and EasyPrivacy (patterns re-written, no lists copied).
  • The QR decoder is original code following ISO/IEC 18004, with techniques described by the ZXing project.
  • Phishing-technique research from public reports by security researchers (SANS ISC, Barracuda, Trustwave, Hoxhunt, KnowBe4, Malware on Tap and others).
